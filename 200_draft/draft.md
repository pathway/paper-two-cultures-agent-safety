# The Two Cultures of Agents. What Reward, Policy, Action, Observation and Transition Mean for LLM Chatbots and Tool Agents

## Abstract

{{fact:scope_purpose}} One culture writes down the decision process and the reward and learns a policy; the other prompts a pretrained model inside a scaffold with tools and judges it by what it does. We take the {{fact:primitives_n}} primitives that @sutton2018 define, reward, policy, action, observation, state, transition, episode and value, and read each one in {{fact:settings_n}} settings: an RL agent in training and in deployment, chat training with an outcome reward model, with a verifier and with a process reward model, agent training with trajectory reward, and deployment of a chatbot, of a tool agent with a human between steps and of an autonomous tool agent. The reading is a table of {{fact:cells_n}} cells, each with its reason, and sources where the reason rests on one. We also code {{fact:properties_n}} properties in every setting and compare settings only within a phase. In training and in deployment alike, LLM agents differ from RL agents on {{fact:culture_all_both_n}} properties, among them that an LLM agent's observations can carry instructions and its behaviour can be steered by instructions in its input. In deployment the RL agent's task still has a reward function and the LLM's has none. Some differences that look cultural are differences of phase: neither a deployed RL policy nor a deployed LLM uses reward to change its parameters. Chat training with a learned or programmatic outcome reward is a contextual bandit with no subsequent decision and a degenerate value, for which the critic is often dropped; process supervision has the code of RL training, where both bandit settings do not, on whether {{fact:prm_like_rl}}, though its per-step reward is a learned score. @breiman2001 described the same split in statistics, between a culture that assumes a stochastic model of the inside of the box and one that treats the inside as unknown and judges by prediction. We set the two cultures of agents in his lines, measure how little the two literatures cite each other, and say which of his discussants' objections transfer: Cox's on prediction under changed conditions and Efron's on prediction being only occasionally sufficient.

## Introduction {#sec:intro}

**Scope.** {{fact:scope_purpose}}

**Position.** The RL primitives do not denote the same objects for LLM chatbots and LLM tool agents as for the agents they were defined for, and the difference is one of setting, not of scale. The same weights are a bandit policy during preference training, a tree policy under process supervision, a frozen function in a chatbot, and one component of a loop with side effects in a tool agent. An RL reader who hears that an agent *hacked its reward* should ask which setting is meant, because a deployed LLM agent's task, unlike a deployed RL agent's, has no reward function to hack. The paper gives that reader a table.

@sutton2018 define the agent–environment interface in a few sentences, and the flexibility they build in, time steps as "arbitrary successive stages of decision making and acting" [@sutton2018], is what lets @mclean2026 write an LLM agent as a POMDP. We agree that the writing is legitimate. We ask what each symbol in it denotes once the setting is fixed, and we read RL itself in both phases, training and deployment, because a deployed RL policy is frozen too and the comparison must not oppose RL training to LLM deployment.

The paper is organised around one table, split by phase (Tables \ref{tab:primitive_cells_training} and \ref{tab:primitive_cells_deployment}). §\ref{sec:vocab} fixes the RL reader's definitions from the textbook. §\ref{sec:settings} fixes the settings, which follow our companion paper [@debunking2026] in splitting training from deployment. §\ref{sec:primitives} reads each primitive across the settings. §\ref{sec:cultures} names the two cultures that have grown around the two readings and sets them in Breiman's lines. §\ref{sec:census} measures contact between the two literatures. §\ref{sec:breiman} says where Breiman's argument transfers and where his discussants' objections do.

## The RL reader's vocabulary {#sec:vocab}

We take the definitions from the textbook, so that an RL reader can check the starting point (Table \ref{tab:primitives}). The reward is "a received scalar signal (called reward)" [@sutton2018], "passing from the environment to the agent" [@sutton2018]; it is the designer's channel for "what you want achieved, not how you want it achieved" [@sutton2018]. "Formally, a policy is a mapping from states to probabilities of selecting each possible action." [@sutton2018] An episode ends in a terminal state "followed by a reset to a standard starting state" [@sutton2018], and the next one begins independently. The dynamics are a function p of the state and action. These are the objects we track.

## Settings {#sec:settings}

Neither "RL" nor "LLM" is one setting. We split by phase, training or deployment, and then by how the decision problem is posed: what one action is, and where the reward, if any, comes from (Table \ref{tab:settings}). The RL agent appears twice, in training and as a deployed fixed policy. The training settings for chat differ by reward source: a learned reward model fit to human preferences [@christiano2017; @ouyang2022], a programmatic verifier of the final answer [@lambert2024; @deepseekai2025], or a process reward model that scores each reasoning step [@uesato2022; @lightman2024]. In agent training, as we define the setting, the reward is task success at the end of a multi-turn run of tool calls; the cited multi-turn RL work also uses milestone, per-step and dense rewards [@zhou2024b]. In deployment no reward changes the policy, and the LLM settings have no reward function for the task; they differ by whether a human sees each step. The companion paper uses the same phase split and a coarser posing [@debunking2026]; our D-AGENT-A and D-AGENT-X refine its D-AGENT, and our T-CHAT and T-RLVR refine its T-CHAT.

- **Reward.** {{fact:contrast_reward_defined}} {{fact:contrast_reward_learning}} The reward model, verifier or process reward model is a training artefact: @mclean2026 say of the process reward model that once training is complete it "is no longer used" [@mclean2026], and note that the reward function "is somewhat different from training (updating the policy) to deployment (freezing the policy)" [@mclean2026]. What stands in its place is the instruction in the context, which any later text in the context can revise, and the user's acceptance after the fact.
- **Action and transition.** In {{fact:bandit_settings}} the whole reply is one action and generation leads directly to a terminal state with no subsequent decision: the problem is a contextual bandit [@ahmadian2024; @xiong2024]; the cited work treats RLHF, and we extend the reading to verifier rewards. PPO treats tokens as actions, but the reward is terminal. RLOO and ReMax show that the bandit view suffices and drop the critic [@ahmadian2024; @li2024]; GRPO also drops the critic [@shao2024]. Under process supervision an action is one reasoning step and the transition is deterministic: the agent is the dynamics. In the agent settings an action is a tool call. In agent training the transition is the response of a training environment that, by the definition of the setting, is reset between episodes (the cited work samples a new task for each episode [@zhou2024b]); in deployment it is what the tool and the world do, which no party fully controls.
- **Observation and state.** In RL the observation is data from a sensor. In the LLM settings the observation is mostly text, and text carries instructions; agents may also receive images and other modalities. In the agent settings tool outputs arrive in the same stream as the user's instructions, and models do not reliably separate the two [@zverev2025; @greshake2023]. The state of a deployed agent includes world state it cannot see and the user's intent; @mclean2026 concede that the agent's inputs "cannot fully capture the true state of the world and the user's intents" [@mclean2026]. In deployment the context window is a bounded, lossy belief.
- **Episode and value.** Every training setting resets by design. Under process supervision any prefix can be resampled; in agent training the environment resets between tasks. {{fact:contrast_reset}} A chatbot's conversation ends, and a tool agent's side effects persist. In deployment no LLM setting uses a learned value function, and a deployed RL policy uses one only when it is value-based (a fixed Q-network, or search with a learned value); in the LLM deployment settings other checks stand in for it: the user's acceptance in D-CHAT, the human who sees each step in D-AGENT-A, and verbal self-checks and retries in D-AGENT-X. Search-augmented agents score continuations by reverting to earlier states, which a live deployment does not allow, and in D-AGENT-X each probe is a real action with persistent effects.

## Two cultures of agents {#sec:cultures}

Two research cultures have grown around the two ends of the table. One begins by positing what is inside the box: an optimiser of a reward R in a decision process the designer wrote down, from which value functions, policy gradients and designed exploration follow [@sutton2018; @schulman2017]. The other treats the inside as unknown: a pretrained model steered by a prompt and a scaffold, judged by what it does on tasks [@yao2023b; @kapoor2024]; where it posits a reward, it is to train the model, and none is defined for the task at deployment (Table \ref{tab:gap}). @breiman2001 described the same split in statistics. His data-modeling culture begins "Assume that the data are generated by the following model" [@breiman2001]; he put "98% of all statisticians" [@breiman2001] in it; and his objection was that "The conclusions are about the model's mechanism, and not about nature's mechanism." [@breiman2001] His algorithmic culture "considers the inside of the box complex and unknown" [@breiman2001] and validates by predictive accuracy. Table \ref{tab:cultures} sets the four cultures in his lines. We call ours *objective-modeling* and *behavior-modeling*, by their move, not their tribe. The mapping runs the RL-agent culture to Breiman's statistics, not to his machine learning: in our reading, the move that matters is writing down a model before looking at behaviour: the RL-agent culture writes down a decision process and a reward, as Breiman's data-modeling culture writes down a stochastic data model. The mapping is of moves, not of people: the same researcher may write down a decision process and a reward in the morning and prompt a tool agent in the afternoon. Our comparisons are between settings: T-RL and D-RL stand for the RL-agent culture, and every chat and agent setting, training included, stands for the LLM-agent culture, although chat and agent training use RL algorithms.

Breiman's objection transfers with the mapping. When an LLM agent is written as an optimiser of an internalised reward and conclusions are drawn from that model, the conclusions are about the model's mechanism. Table \ref{tab:settings} says where the designer writes down the decision process and the reward (the RL settings), where the reward is a learned score at each reasoning step (process supervision), where the problem is a bandit (outcome-rewarded chat training), and where the task has no reward function (every LLM deployment setting, unlike a deployed RL agent's task).

## Where Breiman's argument transfers {#sec:breiman}

In Breiman's account the algorithmic culture validates a model by predictive accuracy, and the data-modeling culture by goodness-of-fit tests and residual examination [@breiman2001]. In the behavior-modeling culture, task-success rates on benchmarks are a principal way to judge agents. Cox's objection transfers directly: "Often the prediction is under quite different conditions from the data" [@breiman2001]. An agent trained in a sandbox that resets, where third parties author tool outputs at most in part, is deployed where nothing resets and third parties can author them; the gap between T-AGENT (Table \ref{tab:primitive_cells_training}) and D-AGENT-X (Table \ref{tab:primitive_cells_deployment}) is the gap Cox names. Efron's transfers: "Prediction by itself is only occasionally sufficient." [@breiman2001] A task-success rate says what the agent did on those tasks and nothing about the mechanism, which is what the objective-modeling culture wants to know. Breiman's own Rashomon Effect transfers too: many functions give about the same error rate, so a benchmark score does not identify the agent behind it [@breiman2001; @damour2021].

## Alternative views {#sec:alternatives}

***The POMDP framing is only a lens.*** @mclean2026 formalize a problem equivalence, which they also call an "approximate equivalence" [@mclean2026], and say that their MDP framing "provides a diagnostic lens for discussing and solving these reward specification problems" [@mclean2026]. As a lens it is legitimate, and our table uses it. The disagreement is about what the symbols denote once the lens is applied to a deployment setting.

***Agentic RL is making the deployment settings look like training.*** Multi-turn RL does bring more of the RL machinery into agent training: ArCHer trains a turn-level value function to aggregate reward over utterances [@zhou2024b]. It does not change a deployment cell: the deployed agent is still frozen, as a deployed RL policy is, but unlike it has no reward function for its task and no designed reset, which a deployed RL agent's task may have. The gap between T-AGENT and D-AGENT-X is the sim-to-real gap of this field.

***Everything here is already known to practitioners.*** Much of it is, in pieces: the bandit reading [@ahmadian2024; @lambert2025], the constrained reading of RLHF [@moskovitz2024] and the KL penalty as a Bayesian prior [@korbak2022], and DeepSeek's decision to apply no neural reward model, outcome-based or process-based, to reasoning tasks, citing its observation that such models are susceptible to reward hacking and the cost of retraining them [@deepseekai2025]. We put the pieces in one table, with a reason for each cell and its sources where it rests on one, so that the next argument about whether an LLM agent is an RL agent can point at a cell.

## Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

Unlike the bandit settings, process supervision matches RL training on whether {{fact:prm_like_rl}}, though its reward is a learned score. Compared within a phase, LLM agents differ from RL agents in every setting in that their observations can carry instructions and their behaviour can be steered by instructions in their input; in deployment their task has no reward function and no designed reset, while a deployed RL agent's task has a reward function and may have a designed reset. Whether reward changes the parameters is a difference of phase, not of culture. Two cultures have grown around the two ends of that table and barely cite each other. Two of Breiman's discussants' objections transfer to them: prediction under changed conditions is the hard case, and a score is only occasionally sufficient.
