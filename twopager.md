## Abstract

Breiman [1] divided statistics into a data-modeling culture, which assumes the data are generated by a stated stochastic model, and an algorithmic culture, which treats the mechanism as unknown and judges models by predictive accuracy. Agent safety has the same split. One culture begins "assume the agent maximizes a reward R" and derives safety from that model: constrained MDPs, shields, safe exploration [2, 3, 4]. The other treats the deployed agent as a black box, measures what it does, and bounds harm by architecture. We show the split is real in the citation graph, and we test each culture's founding assumption against two sets of real LLM-agent incidents. The reward-model culture's taxonomy of failures has no slot for about half of the incidents, and the slot it assigns is unstable under rewording while the facts are stable. The behaviour culture's claim that nothing can be specified is also wrong: in every deployer-operated incident a written rule could have blocked the first-order harm. But the rule lives in the tool layer as access control, not in the agent's objective. We draw Hoadley's conclusion from the Breiman discussion [1]: black boxes run in production only inside written constraints at the boundary. The open problem is the measured residual: concealment, legitimate-looking injected instructions, and gates that operators switch off.

## 1. The same move, twice

Breiman's data-modeling culture is defined by one sentence: "Assume that the data are generated by the following model" [1, §4.1]. He objected that "the conclusions are about the model's mechanism, and not about nature's mechanism" [1, §5]. McLean et al. [5] write an LLM agent as a POMDP, hypothesize that the frozen policy has "internalized" a reward f(o_p, o_g), and conclude that agent failures are instances of RL failure classes. That is the same move. The conclusions are about the reward model's mechanism, not the agent's. We call the two cultures *objective-modeling* and *behavior-modeling*, by their move, not their tribe. The mapping runs RL to Breiman's statistics, not to his machine learning, because the move that matters is positing a parametric model of the inside of the box. In robotics RL is the algorithmic culture against control; here it inherits control's assumption that R and the constraint set are given [3, 4].

Table 1 states each culture's assumptions in Breiman's format, with the incident rate at which each assumption fails `[U]`.

|---|---|---|
| Inside the box | an optimizer of a reward R that the designer knows, or that the agent has "internalized" | unknown: a pretrained artifact with latent dispositions, lightly steered |

## 3. Testing the objective-modeling culture on incidents

We assembled 70 source-verified LLM-agent incidents (24 deployed harms, 46 exploits of shipped products) [8] and had an LLM judge code each on 24 dimensions drawn from 15 taxonomies, with a reworded second pass for stability `[U]`.

- McLean et al.'s three classes (reward misspecification, unsafe action in an irreversible state, acting on an insufficient observation) fit 53% of incidents; **47% fit none**. Reward misspecification, the flagship class, covers 10%.
- Of 50 injection-triggered incidents, 28 fit no class and 20 were filed as "insufficient observation". The filing hides that the observation rewrote the objective [9].
- The class label is unstable: kappa 0.25 between the two prompts, while trigger, gate status and severity are stable at kappa 0.86–0.90. The reworded judge moved five injection cases from "insufficient observation" to "reward misspecification". The class assignment is post hoc, and this is a measurement of it.
- On a unified AI-safety taxonomy, the RL-native problem areas (reward hacking, side effects, safe exploration) together cover at most 20% of incidents; adversarial inputs and injection cover 70%.

Table 1 shows which assumptions the incidents break. The LLM-side rows dominate; the row the behavior culture leans on hardest, that the unsafe set cannot be written, is implicated in only 9% `[U]`, which anticipates §4. The full 23-row table is in the companion file `assumptions_table.md`.

## 4. Testing the behavior-modeling culture on incidents

The behavior culture's strong form says the unsafe set cannot be written before deployment [10, §6]. We asked, for 148 deduplicated incidents from the AI Incident Database and our catalog, whether a designer could have written a concrete, machine-checkable rule in advance that would have blocked the harmful action `[U]`.

| Could a pre-deployment rule have blocked first-order harm? | All, deduplicated (N=148) | Deployer-operated, AIID set (N=64) | Deployer-operated, catalog (N=49) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yes | 46% | 61% | 65% |
| Partial (first-order harm blockable, rest not) | 45% | 39% | 35% |
| No | 9% | **0%** | **0%** |

Every "no" is an attacker-operated agent, where no deployer existed to write a rule. So the strong claim is false. But the rules that would have worked are not what the objective culture builds. Of 154 writable rules, 153 are tool-layer access control: allow/deny lists (78%), approval gates (61–75%), least-privilege credentials, egress default-deny, sandboxes. **One** is a cost over the agent's policy. Both raters placed all 70 catalog incidents under the "control, monitoring, runtime defence" area of the unified taxonomy; none under formal guarantees or specification.

## 5. What the residual is

The partial cases and the disabled gates are the paper's open problem. Concealment is present in 14% of incidents and is fully blockable in 1 of 15 `[U]`. In 34% a human gate was present and was bypassed by attack or disabled by the operator; the Terraform case ran with permission prompts switched off [11]. Injected instructions that look legitimate are blockable only at the cost of blocking legitimate instructions. These are failures of rows the assumptions table marks as LLM-specific: the objective arrives over the observation channel, the agent models its overseer, the human in the loop removes friction.

## 6. Where Breiman's argument does and does not transfer

Breiman had a shared scoreboard, test-set accuracy. Agent safety has none; rare catastrophic events are where both cultures are weakest. Cox's objection transfers: "often the prediction is under quite different conditions from the data" [1, Cox]. Efron's transfers: "prediction by itself is only occasionally sufficient" [1, Efron]; a passed eval has no confidence interval over deployments, and evals are specifications too [12]. The analogue that holds is Hoadley's. Fair Isaac ran black-box scorecards in production for decades, inside "score engineering" constraints that clients, law and drift demanded [1, Hoadley]. That is what the incident data say about agents: a black box judged by behaviour, wrapped in written access control at the boundary. The objective culture is right that constraints work and wrong about where they live. The behavior culture is right that the objective cannot be specified and wrong if it concludes nothing can be written.

## 7. Related work

No prior paper sets these two cultures against each other. The nearest are Turner's argument that RL policies need not maximize reward [13], Çağatan and Zhao's port of the RL safety gridworlds to LLM agents, where specification gaming appears without task training [14], and Dantas et al.'s survey finding specification to be the bottleneck while never mentioning safe RL [15]. The ethics-versus-safety binary in AI is already discussed as a pair of cultures [16]; ours is orthogonal to it. Breiman's own sequels supply the tools we borrow: Shmueli's explain-or-predict distinction [17], Donoho's Common Task Framework as the predictive culture's engine [12], and the Rashomon-set literature, which says an eval suite does not pin down the model behind it [18]. Three agent-incident databases predate our catalog [8]; we add the trigger, gate-status and assumption-row fields and the measurements built on them.

## 8. Conclusion

Two cultures, a measured gap, and one bridge named "reward hacking". The reward-model picture does not describe deployed incidents and its class labels do not survive rewording. Tool-layer rules would have blocked first-order harm in every deployer-operated case, and almost none of those rules is a cost function. The paper's recommendation is Hoadley's: engineer the boundary, measure the box, and treat the residual, concealment, legitimate-looking injection, and switched-off gates, as the research problem neither culture owns.
